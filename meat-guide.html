COOKING GUIDE

Cooking Meat Well

A practical guide to choosing the right method for the cut, seasoning it properly, managing heat, and knowing when it is done.

FIRST PRINCIPLE

Match the method to the cut.

Tender cuts usually benefit from fast, dry heat. Tough, collagen-rich cuts become tender with time and moisture. Thick steaks are easier to cook evenly with a two-stage method; thin cutlets are better with fast pan heat.

Dry Aging

What it does: controlled aging concentrates flavor and allows natural enzymes to tenderize beef.

  • Wet-aged: aged in vacuum packaging; milder flavor and less moisture loss.
  • Dry-aged: held under tightly controlled refrigeration, airflow, and humidity; develops deeper nutty, beefy flavor and loses moisture.
  • Best use: thick steaks and roasts where the added flavor is noticeable.

Important: true dry-aging requires controlled equipment and food-safety management. Do not improvise long-term dry-aging in a normal household refrigerator.

Why Salting Matters

Salt is more than surface seasoning. Given time, it dissolves in surface moisture and diffuses inward, seasoning the meat more evenly and helping it retain moisture during cooking.

  • Thin cuts: salt shortly before cooking.
  • Steaks and chops: salt at least 40 minutes ahead, or ideally several hours to overnight uncovered in the refrigerator.
  • Large roasts: season 12–24 hours ahead when practical.

Pat the surface dry before searing. A dry surface browns faster.

Core Cooking Methods

Grilling

Best for steaks, chops, burgers, sausages, chicken, and thinner cuts. Use direct heat for browning and indirect heat to finish thicker pieces. Avoid constant flipping over flare-ups; move meat to a cooler zone when needed.

Pan-Searing

Ideal for steaks, chops, cutlets, and small tender pieces. Preheat the pan, dry the meat well, use a thin layer of high-heat fat, and avoid crowding.

Reverse Sear

Excellent for thick steaks and roasts. Cook gently in a low oven or indirect grill until the center is near target temperature, then finish with a very hot sear for crust.

Sous Vide

Vacuum-sealed meat is held in a precisely controlled water bath, then dried thoroughly and seared. It gives exceptionally even doneness and is especially useful for thick steaks, pork chops, and tougher cuts cooked for extended periods.

Roasting

Best for larger tender cuts and whole poultry. High heat promotes browning; moderate heat gives more even doneness. Use a thermometer rather than time alone.

Braising

Brown the meat, add aromatics and a modest amount of liquid, cover, and cook gently until collagen converts to gelatin. Ideal for chuck, short ribs, shanks, shoulder, and similar cuts.

Slow Cooking

Low, steady heat works well for stews, ragùs, pulled meats, and collagen-rich cuts. Browning first usually improves flavor even when the long cook happens in a slow cooker.

Smoking

Combines low heat, time, and wood smoke. Best for brisket, ribs, pork shoulder, and other cuts that benefit from extended cooking. Manage airflow and temperature rather than chasing heavy smoke.

The Sear

Browning comes from the Maillard reaction. For a strong crust: dry the surface, use enough heat, avoid crowding, and give the meat time in contact with the pan or grill. Sugary marinades can burn before the meat browns, so wipe off excess marinade before very high heat.

Temperature & Carryover

An instant-read thermometer is the most reliable way to judge doneness. Thick meat continues to rise in temperature after leaving the heat, so pull it slightly before the final target when appropriate.

MeatPractical guidance
Whole cuts of beef, veal, lambChoose doneness by preference; allow for carryover and rest before slicing.
Ground meatsCook more thoroughly than intact steaks because grinding distributes surface bacteria throughout.
PorkModern pork does not need to be cooked until dry; use a thermometer and rest appropriately.
PoultryCook to a safe endpoint; thighs tolerate higher finishing temperatures than breasts and often become more tender there.

Resting & Slicing

Resting lets temperature equalize and reduces juice loss when meat is cut. Small steaks may need only a few minutes; large roasts need longer. Slice across the grain when possible to shorten muscle fibers and make meat easier to chew.

Which Method Should I Use?

  • Thick ribeye, strip, porterhouse: reverse sear, sous vide + sear, or two-zone grill.
  • Thin steak or cutlet: hot pan or direct grill.
  • Chuck, shank, short ribs: braise or slow cook.
  • Brisket, ribs, pork shoulder: smoke or slow cook.
  • Whole chicken: roast, indirect grill, or smoke.
  • Chicken breast: pan-sear, grill, roast, or sous vide; avoid overcooking.
  • Ground beef: hot griddle, skillet, or grill; handle gently and do not overwork.
Food-safety note: raw and undercooked meat can carry foodborne-illness risk. Use reliable refrigeration, avoid cross-contamination, and use appropriate safe time-and-temperature practices—especially for poultry, ground meat, and sous vide cooking.
